OVERVIEW

Project overview

This case study examines a cleaned copy of Global Superstore, a public retail dataset widely used in Tableau training and shared on Kaggle, and presents the analysis through an interactive browser dashboard. The focus is sales and profitability across time, markets, categories, sub-categories and discount levels.

02

SCOPE

Business questions

  • Which markets and categories generate the most sales?
  • Which markets and categories generate the most profit?
  • What is the overall profit margin?
  • Which sub-categories reduce profitability?
  • How are discount levels associated with profit?
  • Which sales lines generate losses?
  • How can the dashboard support further investigation?
03

DATA

Dataset and context

The source is orders_clean.csv, a cleaned export of the public Global Superstore dataset with 51,290 rows dated 2011–2014. Relevant fields include order date, market, category, sub-category, sales, profit and discount. Figures on this page use the full dataset with no dashboard filters applied.

04

ANALYSIS

How the analysis was structured

The work moves from overall sales, profit and margin to comparisons by market, category and sub-category, then to discount bands and loss-making sales lines. The SQL script covers the data checks and the category, Furniture sub-category and Tables discount questions. The dashboard is a separate piece: it does not run SQL, but reads a summary of the same CSV and recalculates every view in JavaScript when filters change.

05

DATA CHECKS

How the data was checked

I ran the SQL script against orders_clean.csv loaded into SQLite 3.37. The only change was the table name (public.orders to orders). The four PostgreSQL-specific statements that inspect and convert date types (queries 12, 13, 15 and 16) were not needed, because the cleaned CSV already stores ISO dates.

  • 01

    51,290 rows, each with a unique Row ID, covering 25,035 orders: one order can contain several product lines, so rows are sales lines, not orders.

  • 02

    Order dates run from 1 January 2011 to 31 December 2014; ship dates end on 7 January 2015.

  • 03

    457 product IDs are linked to more than one product name (15 of them in Tables). For this reason the analysis groups by sub-category and market rather than by product ID.

  • 04

    The SQL totals (sales $12,642,501.91, profit $1,467,457.29) match the unfiltered dashboard values.

06

DASHBOARD

Dashboard design

The dashboard is built with HTML, CSS and JavaScript and has three views: Overview, Profitability and Discounts. Year, market, category and sub-category filters update every chart and KPI.

Overview shows sales and margin by year, profit by market and by category. Profitability ranks all 17 sub-categories by profit, with losses in red. Discounts uses the same five discount levels as this page (no discount, under 20%, 20–29%, 30–49%, 50% or more), a market by discount heatmap, and the Tables lines at 30% or more that the pilot below would target.

08

OBSERVATIONS

Main observations

Observed in the dataset

  • 01

    APAC records the highest market sales ($3,585,744.13) and profit ($436,000.05).

  • 02

    Technology has the highest category sales ($4,744,557.50) and profit ($663,778.73).

  • 03

    Tables is the only sub-category with negative aggregate profit: −$64,083.39.

  • 04

    Sales lines with discounts of 30% or more total $1,736,588.11 in sales (13.7% of the total) and −$813,212.32 in profit, an amount equal to 55% of the total reported profit.

  • 05

    Margin falls at every discount level: 25.3% with no discount, 15.6% below 20%, 8.3% at 20–29%, −23.3% at 30–49% and −82.1% at 50% or more.

  • 06

    The pattern holds like for like. In all 92 sub-category and market combinations with at least 10 sales lines in each group, lines discounted at 30% or more lose money, while lines below 30% are profitable.

  • 07

    Tables below a 30% discount earns $68,457.76 (16.2% margin) and is profitable in every market. At 30% or more it loses −$132,541.15, and loses money in every market where such discounts occur.

  • 08

    EMEA applies discounts of 30% or more on 32% of its sales lines, the highest share of any market, and has the lowest market margin (5.45%).

Interpretation

The losses follow deep discounts more closely than any single product or market. Tables looks less like an unprofitable product than a product that is often sold at loss-making discount levels. The data does not show why those discounts were granted.

09

RECOMMENDATIONS

What I would propose

These are proposals to validate with sales and finance stakeholders, not conclusions the historical data can prove on its own.

  • 01

    Add an approval step for discounts of 30% or more. Deep discounts would need a manager’s sign-off and a reason code (for example clearance, competitive deal or key account). This keeps flexibility while making each loss-making discount a deliberate decision.

  • 02

    Pilot it on Tables first. Start in APAC, the US and the EU, where deep-discount Tables lines lose the most (−$37,917.59, −$30,698.22 and −$28,162.96). Review EMEA’s discount policy in parallel, given its share of deep discounts.

  • 03

    Measure the pilot against a control group. Track margin, sales volume and the share of deals closed. The $813,212.32 lost on deep discounts is an upper bound for the benefit: some of those sales would not happen at a lower discount.

  • 04

    Collect the missing data. Product cost, promotion reason and customer type are needed to separate justified discounts from avoidable ones.

10

LIMITS

Limitations

  • The data covers 2011–2014 and does not describe current performance.
  • Aggregate comparisons do not control for product mix, order size, customer type, costs or other variables.
  • The observed discount-profit relationship is descriptive; it does not establish that discounts caused lower profit.
11

NEXT QUESTIONS

Open questions

  • Why were deep discounts granted: clearance, competitive deals or specific customers?
  • How would sales volume respond to an approval step? This needs a controlled pilot rather than historical data.
  • What cost, promotion and customer data would help explain the differences?
  • Which segments should be prioritised for a deeper review with business stakeholders?
